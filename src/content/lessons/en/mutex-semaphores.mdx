<Stage stage="theory">

A **semaphore** is a counter that never goes below 0, plus a queue of blocked threads. It has two operations, and each one is indivisible:

- `wait(s)`: if `s > 0`, subtract 1 and the thread continues. If `s = 0`, the thread **blocks** in the queue of `s` and gives up the CPU.
- `signal(s)`: if a thread is blocked on `s`, wake the first one: its `wait` completes and it joins the ready queue; the counter does not change. If nobody is waiting, add 1.

Think of the counter as a number of permits. `wait` takes one or waits until there is one; `signal` returns one and, if someone is waiting, hands it over directly.

A semaphore that starts at 1 and is always used as `wait … signal` around a critical section gives **mutual exclusion**: it is a **mutex**.

The **bounded buffer** has *N* slots used in a circle. Producers write at slot `in` and consumers read at slot `out`; both indexes advance one at a time and wrap back to 0 at the end. Writing a slot and advancing `in` is a critical section, just like `counter++`. The solution uses three semaphores:

| Semaphore | Counts | Starts at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `empty` | free slots | *N* |
| `full` | filled slots | 0 |
| `mutex` | permission to touch the indexes | 1 |

```
producer:              consumer:
  wait(empty)            wait(full)
  wait(mutex)            wait(mutex)
  put item               take item
  signal(mutex)          signal(mutex)
  signal(full)           signal(empty)
```

> Example: `s = 1`. Thread A does `wait(s)`: `s` becomes 0 and A continues. Thread B does `wait(s)`: B blocks. A does `signal(s)`: B wakes up holding the permit and `s` stays at 0.

<Stage stage="explain">

The right answers were: **P1 blocks first, on `empty`**, and **`empty` holds 0** after the first `signal(empty)`.

**Blocking is not spinning.** P1 fills both slots and at step 11 its `wait(empty)` finds 0. It does not overwrite anything and it does not keep checking in a loop: it joins the queue of `empty` and gives up the CPU. A blocked thread costs nothing until someone wakes it. If you thought C1 would block first because the buffer starts empty, notice that C1 does not even get the CPU until P1 blocks.

**`signal` does not always add.** At step 16, C1 frees a slot with `signal(empty)`, but P1 is waiting on `empty`. The permit goes straight to P1, which wakes up, and the counter stays at 0. At step 21, on the other hand, nobody is waiting on `empty` (P1 is already ready) and the counter rises to 1.

**Two kinds of waiting.** `empty` and `full` make threads wait for a **condition**: room or data. `mutex` makes them wait for **exclusive access**. A mutex alone protects the indexes, but it cannot wait for room: the producer would have to release the lock, look again and again, and burn CPU.

**The order of the waits matters.** If the producer does `wait(mutex)` before `wait(empty)` and finds the buffer full, it blocks **while holding the mutex**. The consumer, the only one that could free a slot, needs the mutex first and blocks too. Nobody can ever `signal`: that is a **deadlock**. The rule: wait for the condition first, then take the mutex, and release it as soon as possible.

**A bug that depends on the interleaving.** The swapped version finishes fine with quantum 1, because the consumer frees a slot before the producer takes the mutex with the buffer full. Like a race condition, a timing-dependent deadlock can hide for thousands of runs.

**POSIX does the same in another order.** Here `signal` hands the permit straight to the waiter. In POSIX, `sem_post` adds 1 and wakes a thread, which subtracts 1 when it runs. Once the woken thread moves on, the counter holds the same value either way.

**A mutex and a semaphore are not the same.** A mutex has an owner: only the thread that locked it may unlock it, which lets the system catch mistakes and apply priority inheritance. A semaphore has no owner: any thread may `signal`, and that is exactly why `full` and `empty` work as messages between producer and consumer.

**In real systems.** POSIX offers `sem_wait` and `sem_post`, and `pthread_mutex_lock` and `pthread_mutex_unlock`; Java has `java.util.concurrent.Semaphore`; Python has `threading.Semaphore` and `queue.Queue`, a ready-made bounded buffer. On Linux, mutexes and semaphores are built on the `futex` system call: when nobody competes for the lock, taking and releasing it never enters the kernel.

<Stage stage="deeper">

- **Condition variables and monitors.** `pthread_cond_wait` and, in Java, `synchronized` with `wait` and `notify` are another way to wait for a condition without spinning.
- **Readers–writers and the dining philosophers.** More classic synchronization problems; the philosophers open the next module, deadlock.
- **Priority inversion.** An urgent thread waits for a mutex held by a low-priority one, which in turn is pushed aside by a medium one. It happened to the Mars Pathfinder probe in 1997; priority inheritance fixes it.
- **Futexes.** How Linux keeps uncontended locks out of the kernel.
